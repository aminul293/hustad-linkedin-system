# Tells caught in practice

`CONTENT_STANDARDS.md` is the standard. This file is the running log of what actually goes wrong
when something drafts in Eric's voice, recorded the day each one was caught, so the same mistake
does not get made twice. Part 1 of the standards names the classic patterns. These are the ones
that slipped past it.

Read this before drafting. The mechanical ones are enforced in `content_engine.voice_lint()`; the
rest need a human eye, which is why they are written out.

## The setup and flip

The banned cadence has a disguise. "X is not Y. It is Z." gets caught. The same rhythm wearing
different clothes does not:

- "Your six make the response better. None of them change how often the phone rings."
- "A phone number catches the call. A system limits the damage."

Two beats, a claim and its reversal, delivered for effect. If a sentence sets something up so the
next one can turn it over, that is the same move. Say the second thing and drop the first.

## The matched pair

Two clauses built to the same length and shape so they balance:

- "If the record starts at the call, that turns into an argument. If there is a graded inspection
  sitting there, it is a comparison."

Real speech is lumpy. Break the symmetry: keep one, rewrite the other as something plainer, or
merge them.

## The bow

A final line that summarizes what was just said and ties it off:

- "Everything you listed gets easier when the baseline already exists."
- "Same format, same account manager, every property."

Stop on the last real thing instead. A comment or a DM that ends on a concrete observation reads
finished. A comment that ends on a summary reads written.

## Puns and polish

- "The temporary versus permanent piece is where most programs leak."
- "nothing closes quietly just because the water stopped."

A clever turn in the middle of an operator sentence is the loudest tell there is. Nobody thinking
out loud lands a pun on their own trade.

## The recycled line

One phrase carried across four consecutive drafts: "starts from somebody's memory instead of a
record." It was good once. By the fourth use it was a signature, which is exactly what item 7 of
the standards warns about. Track your own phrasing across a session and retire anything that has
already been used.

## Wrong altitude

The worst failure in this log. Karl Baedeker posted a systems piece on emergency planning doctrine,
and the draft answered with roof hatches, lift access and who holds the key. All true, all useless.
He writes about organizational design; the reply put Eric at the level of a field supervisor.

Match the altitude of the person being answered. An operator writing about authority and escalation
gets an answer about volume, aging and where load comes from. Tactical detail belongs further down
the conversation, after somebody asks for it.

## Signposting, the quiet version

The standards ban "here is the part that gets missed." The softer forms slip through:

- "The part that actually costs you is..."
- "The one thing intake still cannot tell you is..."
- "That is the question the adjuster asks."

All of them announce that something important is coming instead of just saying it. Delete the
frame, keep the content.

## Cute structural gestures

Offering someone a numbered "seventh question" to append to their six. Building a reply that mirrors
the structure of the post it answers. These read as constructed because they are. Contribute an
idea, not a format.

## Listing the obvious

A comment that lists items every reader already knows adds nothing and costs credibility. If the
draft is a list of things a competent person in that role does daily, it is not an insight. Cut it
and find the thing they have not considered.
